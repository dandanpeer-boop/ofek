Sleep

The short answer: Adults need at least 7 hours of sleep a night, according to the AASM and SRS consensus statement. People who slept too little had a 12% higher mortality risk in a meta-analysis of 16 studies.

Sleep is not wasted time. The good news: chronic sleep problems have an effective treatment without medication.

How many hours

According to the consensus statement of the American Academy of Sleep Medicine and the Sleep Research Society (2015), a healthy adult needs at least 7 hours of sleep a night. 6 hours or less is not enough to maintain health and safety.

In Cappuccio's meta-analysis (2010), short sleep was linked to 12% higher mortality risk, and very long sleep to 30%. Very long sleep is often a sign of illness rather than its cause, so this is not a recommendation to sleep less.

Regularity matters as much as quantity

A fixed wake-up time, weekends included, is the anchor of the body clock. If you want to go to bed earlier, it is better to shift gradually, a quarter of an hour at a time.

How to sleep better

Caffeine: in Drake's trial (2013), 400 mg of caffeine, roughly the amount in four cups of coffee, harmed sleep even when taken six hours before bedtime, including on an objective measurement. So the advice is to avoid significant caffeine in the last six hours before sleep.

Screens and light: Chang (2015) compared reading on a light-emitting e-book with a printed book in the hours before bed. Screen readers fell asleep more slowly, secreted less melatonin, had their body clock shifted later, and were less alert the next morning. So it is worth keeping bright screens away for the last hour before sleep, or switching to a printed book.

Surroundings and evening habits: according to advice from the US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC), aim for a quiet, dark bedroom at a comfortable temperature, with no screens or phone by the bed, no heavy meal or alcohol in the hours before sleep, and physical activity during the day. In the morning, it is worth getting some daylight. These are general sleep hygiene tips, not the result of a particular trial.

When falling asleep is hard: in Trauer's meta-analysis (2015)ⓘ20 randomised trials, 1,162 participants with chronic insomnia, cognitive behavioural therapy (CBT-I) included at least three of these components: cognitive therapy, stimulus control (the bed is used only for sleep), sleep restriction, sleep hygiene and relaxation. It shortened the time to fall asleep by about 19 minutesⓘ95% CI: 14–24, reduced night-time wakefulness by about 26 minutes, and raised sleep efficiency by about 10 percentage points. Total sleep time barely changed (about 8 minutes, not significant). In other words, the treatment improves the quality of sleep, not necessarily its length.

How to sleep longer

Someone who sleeps less than 7 hours needs to move their bedtime earlier in practice, not just wish to sleep more. In Tasali's trial (2022), 80 adults aged 21–40 with overweight, who slept under 6.5 hours a night, took part. Half of them received one individual sleep-hygiene counselling session, aimed at reaching 8.5 hours in bed. They slept about 1.2 hours more per night on averageⓘ95% CI: 1.0–1.4 than the group that changed nothing, and ate about 270 fewer calories a dayⓘ95% CI: 147–393, without being told to change their diet.

This was a trial in young adults with overweight, so the result may not be the same for everyone. To move bedtime earlier in practice: choose a fixed wake-up time, count back from it the number of hours you want to sleep and add time to fall asleep, and move your bedtime earlier gradually, a quarter of an hour at a time.

When sleep won't come

According to the American College of Physicians guideline (2016), the first treatment for chronic insomnia is cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT-I), before medication. It is available face to face, in a group, through an app or from a book. Loud snoring, pauses in breathing or severe daytime tiredness are a reason to see your doctor.

Sleep apnea

Obstructive sleep apnoea is repeated pauses in breathing during the night. In the meta-analysis by Fu (2017)ⓘ27 cohort studies; the comparison by severity was based on 11 studies, severe apnoea was linked to about 2.1 times the risk of death from any cause compared with people without apnoeaⓘHR 2.13, 95% confidence interval: 1.68–2.68. The authors note publication bias and only partial adjustment for risk factors in some studies. For mild and moderate apnoea the link was not statistically significantⓘ1.19 for mild, 1.28 for moderate.

In the meta-analysis by Benjafield (2025)ⓘ30 studies, ten of them randomised trials, positive airway pressure treatment (PAP, including CPAP) was linked to 37% lower all-cause mortality compared with no treatment or little useⓘHR 0.63, 95% confidence interval: 0.56–0.72. In the randomised trials alone there was no statistically significant differenceⓘ0.87, 95% confidence interval: 0.65–1.16. The overall significance came from non-randomised studies, so part of the difference may be due to other differences between people who are treated and people who are not. A trend toward greater benefit with more use was seen, but the difference was not significant. The authors describe this as a possible effect, and the study was funded by ResMed, a maker of PAP devices.

Sleep apnea is not included in the calculation. Loud snoring, breathing pauses that someone has noticed, or severe tiredness during the day are a reason to see a family doctor, who can refer for a sleep study.

Sleeping pills: what to know

Prescription sleep medicines suit some people, as a decision made with a doctor. These are official warnings from the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA), not a call to avoid them in every case.

Zolpidem, eszopiclone and zaleplon: in 2019 the FDA added a boxed warning about complex sleep behaviors, such as sleepwalking or sleep-driving, that caused serious injuries and deaths. The events happened even at the lowest recommended dose and after a single dose, and the person usually does not remember them. Anyone who notices such behavior should stop the medicine and contact a doctor.

Benzodiazepines: in 2020 the FDA updated the boxed warning for the whole class: risk of misuse, addiction, physical dependence and withdrawal reactions. Combining them with opioids or alcohol raises the risk of overdose and death. They should not be stopped all at once but tapered gradually with a doctor, because withdrawal can include seizures.

Before any medicine, it is worth asking about CBT-I, the recommended first treatment.

Supplements for sleep: what the evidence shows

Many people feel relief from supplements, and that can be real. But personal experience cannot tell a supplement apart from the placebo effect or from another change that happened at the same time, so only what was tested in trials appears here.

Melatonin: in the meta-analysis by Ferracioli-Oda (2013)ⓘ19 trials, 1,683 participants with primary sleep disorders, melatonin shortened the time to fall asleep by about 7 minutes compared with placeboⓘ95% CI: 4.4–9.8 and lengthened total sleep time by about 8 minutesⓘ95% CI: 1.7–14.8. The authors call the effect modest, and note that it did not fade with continued use.

Magnesium: in a review by Mah (2021)ⓘthree trials, 151 older adults with insomnia, sleep onset time was shortened by about 17 minutes compared with placeboⓘ95% confidence interval: 7.4–27.3, with no significant improvement in total sleep time. All the trials were at moderate to high risk of bias, and the evidence was of low to very low quality, so the authors conclude that the quality of the literature is too low for an informed recommendation. They add that because magnesium is cheap and widely available, the evidence may support its use for insomnia symptoms.

L-theanine: in the trial by Hidese (2019), 30 adults received 200 mg a day for four weeks in a placebo-controlled crossover design. Scores on the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI) fell (that is, improved), mainly in sleep latency, sleep disturbances and use of sleep medication. It is a small trial that mainly examined stress and cognition, and sleep was a secondary outcome.

Glycine: a review by Bannai and Kawai (2012) reports that glycine before bedtime improved subjective sleep quality in people with insomnia tendencies. The evidence comes from small trials by the same research group.

Chamomile, the natural source of apigenin: in a meta-analysis of 10 trials (2024)ⓘ772 participants, the PSQI score fell by 1.88 pointsⓘ95% CI: 0.31–3.46, with high heterogeneity, but sleep duration and sleep efficiency did not improve.

Supplements with no controlled trials for sleep: apigenin alone and phosphatidylserine have not been tested in a randomised controlled trial in adults (the only trial on apigenin tested a mixture of several ingredients). For myo-inositol there is one trial in pregnant women (2022) and none in the general population. That means there is no evidence yet, not that they do not work. There is also no known research on a combination of all these supplements together.

The American Academy of Sleep Medicine guideline (Sateia 2017) suggests, as a weak recommendation, not using melatonin, valerian or tryptophan to treat chronic insomnia in adults, compared with no treatment. The recommendation on melatonin was based on trials of 2 mg, including three trials only in adults over 55, and the quality of evidence for it was rated very low. A weak recommendation reflects lower certainty, and does not mean the supplement does not work. According to other Academy guidelines on which this guideline relies, anyone with chronic insomnia should receive CBT-I as the first treatment.

If a particular supplement helps you, there is no conclusion here that you should stop. It is only worth checking with a doctor or pharmacist, especially if you take regular medication, are pregnant, or the sleep problem is new and sudden.

Myth or fact

"You can "catch up" on sleep at the weekend"What do we know?

What we know: Catching up helps you feel better, but irregular sleep disrupts the body clock. A steady amount every night is better.

"A sleeping pill is the answer to insomnia"What do we know?

What we know: According to the American College of Physicians guideline (2016), the first treatment for chronic insomnia is cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT-I). A medicine comes into consideration only if CBT-I alone did not work, as a shared decision with a doctor, and for a short period.
