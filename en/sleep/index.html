Sleep

The short answer: Adults need at least 7 hours of sleep a night, according to the AASM and SRS consensus statement. People who slept too little had a 12% higher mortality risk in a meta-analysis of 16 studies.

Sleep is not wasted time. Adults need at least 7 hours a night, regularly, and the link between short sleep and mortality shows up in large studies. The good news: chronic sleep problems have an effective treatment without medication.

How many hours

According to the consensus statement of the American Academy of Sleep Medicine and the Sleep Research Society (2015), a healthy adult needs at least 7 hours of sleep a night. 6 hours or less is not enough to maintain health and safety.

In Cappuccio's meta-analysis (2010), short sleep was linked to 12% higher mortality risk, and very long sleep to 30%. Very long sleep is often a sign of illness rather than its cause, so this is not a recommendation to sleep less.

Regularity matters as much as quantity

A fixed wake-up time, weekends included, is the anchor of the body clock. If you want to go to bed earlier, it is better to shift gradually, a quarter of an hour at a time.

How to sleep better

Caffeine: in Drake's trial (2013), 400 mg of caffeine, roughly the amount in four cups of coffee, harmed sleep even when taken six hours before bedtime, including on an objective measurement. So the advice is to avoid significant caffeine in the last six hours before sleep.

Screens and light: Chang (2015) compared reading on a light-emitting e-book with a printed book in the hours before bed. Screen readers fell asleep more slowly, secreted less melatonin, had their body clock shifted later, and were less alert the next morning. So it is worth keeping bright screens away for the last hour before sleep, or switching to a printed book.

When falling asleep is hard: in Trauer's meta-analysis (2015), 20 randomised trials with 1,162 participants with chronic insomnia, cognitive behavioural therapy (CBT-I) included at least three of these components: cognitive therapy, stimulus control (the bed is used only for sleep), sleep restriction, sleep hygiene and relaxation. It shortened the time to fall asleep by about 19 minutes (95% CI: 14–24), reduced night-time wakefulness by about 26 minutes, and raised sleep efficiency by about 10 percentage points. Total sleep time barely changed (about 8 minutes, not significant). In other words, the treatment improves the quality of sleep, not necessarily its length.

How to sleep longer

Someone who sleeps less than 7 hours needs to move their bedtime earlier in practice, not just wish to sleep more. In Tasali's trial (2022), 80 adults aged 21–40 with overweight, who slept under 6.5 hours a night, took part. Half of them received one individual sleep-hygiene counselling session, aimed at reaching 8.5 hours in bed. They slept about 1.2 hours more per night on average (95% CI: 1.0–1.4) than the group that changed nothing, and ate about 270 fewer calories a day (95% CI: 147–393), without being told to change their diet.

This was a trial in young adults with overweight, so the result may not be the same for everyone. To move bedtime earlier in practice: choose a fixed wake-up time, count back from it the number of hours you want to sleep and add time to fall asleep, and move your bedtime earlier gradually, a quarter of an hour at a time.

When sleep won't come

According to the American College of Physicians guideline (2016), the first treatment for chronic insomnia is cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT-I), before medication. It is available face to face, in a group, through an app or from a book. Loud snoring, pauses in breathing or severe daytime tiredness are a reason to see your doctor.

Sleep apnea

Obstructive sleep apnea means repeated pauses in breathing during the night. In the meta-analysis by Fu (2017), 27 cohort studies with 3.16 million participants, severe apnea was linked to about 2.1 times the risk of death from any cause (HR 2.13, 95% CI: 1.68–2.68). For mild (1.19) and moderate (1.28) apnea the link was not statistically significant.

In the meta-analysis by Benjafield (2025), 30 studies, ten of them randomized trials, treatment with continuous positive airway pressure (CPAP) was linked to 37% lower all-cause mortality compared with no treatment (HR 0.63, 95% CI: 0.56–0.72), and the benefit grew with use. The authors describe this as a potential effect. Most studies were not randomized trials, so part of the difference may come from other differences between people who are treated and people who are not.

Sleep apnea is not included in the calculation. Loud snoring, breathing pauses that someone has noticed, or severe tiredness during the day are a reason to see a family doctor, who can refer for a sleep study.

Sleeping pills: what to know

Prescription sleep medicines suit some people, as a decision made with a doctor. These are official warnings from the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA), not a call to avoid them in every case.

Zolpidem, eszopiclone and zaleplon: in 2019 the FDA added a boxed warning about complex sleep behaviors, such as sleepwalking or sleep-driving, that caused serious injuries and deaths. The events happened even at the lowest recommended dose and after a single dose, and the person usually does not remember them. Anyone who notices such behavior should stop the medicine and contact a doctor.

Benzodiazepines: in 2020 the FDA updated the boxed warning for the whole class: risk of misuse, addiction, physical dependence and withdrawal reactions. Combining them with opioids or alcohol raises the risk of overdose and death. They should not be stopped all at once but tapered gradually with a doctor, because withdrawal can include seizures.

Before any medicine, it is worth asking about CBT-I, the recommended first treatment.

Supplements for sleep: what the evidence shows

Many people feel relief from supplements, and that can be real. But personal experience cannot tell a supplement apart from the placebo effect or from another change that happened at the same time, so only what was tested in trials appears here.

Melatonin: in the meta-analysis by Ferracioli-Oda (2013), 19 trials with 1,683 participants with primary sleep disorders, melatonin shortened the time to fall asleep by about 7 minutes compared with placebo (95% CI: 4.4–9.8) and lengthened total sleep time by about 8 minutes (95% CI: 1.7–14.8). The authors call the effect modest, and note that it did not fade with continued use.

Magnesium: in the review by Mah (2021), three trials with 151 older adults with insomnia, the time to fall asleep was about 17 minutes shorter than with placebo (95% CI: 7.4–27.3), with no significant improvement in total sleep time. All trials were at moderate to high risk of bias and the evidence was of low to very low quality, so the authors conclude there is not yet enough basis for an informed recommendation.

L-theanine: in the trial by Hidese (2019), 30 adults received 200 mg a day for four weeks in a placebo-controlled crossover design. Scores on the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI) fell (that is, improved), mainly in sleep latency, sleep disturbances and use of sleep medication. It is a small trial that mainly examined stress and cognition, and sleep was a secondary outcome.

Glycine: a review by Bannai and Kawai (2012) reports that glycine before bedtime improved subjective sleep quality in people with insomnia tendencies. The evidence comes from small trials by the same research group.

Chamomile, the natural source of apigenin: in a meta-analysis of 10 trials with 772 participants (2024), the PSQI score fell by 1.88 points (95% CI: 0.31–3.46, with high heterogeneity), but sleep duration and sleep efficiency did not improve. A PubMed search on 22 November 2026 found no controlled trial of apigenin alone for sleep; the only trial that came up tested a mixture of several ingredients.

Phosphatidylserine and myo-inositol: a PubMed search on 22 November 2026 found no randomized controlled trial of phosphatidylserine for sleep in adults. For myo-inositol there is one trial in pregnant women (2022) and none in the general population. That means there is no evidence yet, not that they do not work. A combination of all these supplements together was also not tested, as far as I found.

The guideline of the American Academy of Sleep Medicine (Sateia 2017) suggests, as a weak recommendation, not using melatonin, valerian or tryptophan to treat chronic insomnia in adults. A weak recommendation means low certainty in the evidence, not that the supplement does not work. The guideline still recommends starting with CBT-I.

If a particular supplement helps you, there is no conclusion here that you should stop. It is only worth checking with a doctor or pharmacist, especially if you take regular medication, are pregnant, or the sleep problem is new and sudden.

Myth or fact

"You can "catch up" on sleep at the weekend"What do we know?

What we know: Catching up helps you feel better, but irregular sleep disrupts the body clock. A steady amount every night is better.

"A sleeping pill is the answer to insomnia"What do we know?

What we know: The professional guideline recommends starting with cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT-I), before medication.
