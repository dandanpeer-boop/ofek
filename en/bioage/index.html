Biological age

The short answer: Biological age (PhenoAge) is calculated from your age and nine values in a blood test. Each year it is higher than your actual age was linked to a 9% higher mortality risk, a hazard ratio of 1.09.

Biological age is a single number that summarizes how "old" your physiology looks on a routine blood test, relative to your actual age. It is not a measure of the clock but of risk: someone whose number is above their age is in a group with higher mortality.

What each value reflects

Each of the nine values says something different about the body, roughly: albumin about nutrition and inflammation, creatinine about kidney function, glucose about sugar metabolism, CRP about inflammation, lymphocyte percentage and the white cell count (WBC) about the immune system, MCV and RDW about the size of red blood cells and the variation between them, and alkaline phosphatase about the liver and bones. The formula did not choose them by biological explanation but by what predicted mortality in the data.

What goes into the calculation

Levine's formula uses age and these nine values from a routine blood test. The weights were derived from a survival model on American data, and the whole formula has been published, including its 2019 correction.

How well it predicts

In Liu's study (PLoS Med 2018), each year of PhenoAge above age was linked to a hazard ratio of 1.09ⓘconfidence interval 1.08–1.10 for all-cause mortality. In predicting 10-year mortality, the AUC of PhenoAge alone was 0.879, versus 0.855 for age alone. So it adds to age, but modestly.

How to calculate it in practice

You need a blood test that includes the nine values, usually a complete blood count and chemistry tests, plus CRP. CRP and RDW sometimes have to be requested explicitly, because they are not always part of a routine test. In the calculator you can upload the test file, and it is read in the browser only, or type the values in manually.

What it is not

It is not a diagnosis and not a measure of your "true age". It is a statistical measure of groups: people with the same value are on average at a certain risk, and an individual can be far from the average. Values vary from test to test, and a test taken during acute illness, for example with a high CRP, will distort the result.

How it enters the calculator

The calculator computes biological age only when all nine values from a 12-month window are present. You can upload a blood-test file and the values are filled in locally in your browser. The difference from actual age enters as one factor, and is capped (see "Why the difference is limited"). When it exists, kidney function and CRP are not counted separately, so that they are not counted twice.

How to read the result

If biological age equals actual age, the risk is average for your age. If it is higher, the risk is higher, and if it is lower, the risk is lower. At a hazard ratio of 1.09 per year, a gap of 5 years is a risk multiple of about 1.5, and a gap of 10 years is a multiple of about 2.4. This is arithmetic that assumes the ratio holds across the range, not a separate finding from the study.

An example of reading

If biological age is 55 and actual age is 50, the gap is 5 years, and in the model that is a risk multiple of about 1.5, before the weakening of the effect with age and the weighting of the other factors.

What the formula does not include

The formula does not include smoking, blood pressure, weight or fitness. Some of them also act through the blood values, so the calculator gives related factors reduced weight so as not to count them twice.

What to do with the result

If biological age is higher than actual age, it is worth checking which value is out of range and discussing it with a doctor. The values themselves are linked to familiar habits such as weight, sugar, activity and sleep, and the guides on the site cover each of them. A single number is a starting point, not a verdict, and a trend across several tests may be more informative.

Why the difference is limited

Extreme blood values, for example because of acute illness, can produce a biological age very far from the actual age. To limit the effect of an outlying value, the calculator limits the difference to 12 years in each direction. This is our limit, not the study's.

Other measures of biological age

There are other measures called "biological age", including epigenetic clocks based on chemical marks on DNA. They are not included in the calculator. PhenoAge was chosen because it is calculated from blood tests that most people already take, and its formula has been published in full.

Biological age is not a single number

The calculator computes biological age from blood tests only. But no single number summarises a person's biological age. There are several signs, and each shows something different: blood, fitness, balance and strength, the body, and the molecular level. We did not find a method tested on mortality that combines all the layers into one number, and there is no established way to weight them. So only a descriptive profile is shown here, with no score and no weights.

What each layer says

Blood. Phenotypic age (PhenoAge) is calculated from nine values in a standard blood test, and it is already included in the site's calculation. In the original study it predicted mortality only slightly better than age alone (area under the curve 0.88 versus 0.86). It was tested in a US population, and a test taken during an acute illness distorts it.

Fitness. Cardiorespiratory fitness is one of the strongest markers studied. In the study by Mandsager (2018), people with very low fitness had a mortality risk 5.04 times higher (confidence interval 4.10–6.20) than people with elite fitness. This is an association, not proof that raising fitness lowers risk, and the authors say so. Between neighbouring fitness groups (below average versus above average) the hazard ratio was 1.41, the same as for smoking in that study, and smoking was measured crudely there. Reference values by age and sex come from the FRIEND study (Kaminsky 2015), for treadmill tests only.

Balance. In the study by Araujo (2022, ages 51–75), people who could not stand on one leg for 10 seconds had a higher mortality risk (hazard ratio 1.84, confidence interval 1.23–2.78). This is a pass-or-fail sign, not a score, so it has no number on the site.

Strength. Weak grip is linked to higher mortality in several large studies (about 1.16 per 5 kg lower in the PURE study, Leong 2015). It is a marker, not a cause: no trial has shown that strengthening grip lowers mortality. The evidence for push-ups is too weak (37 events in male firefighters, and a heart outcome rather than mortality), so they have no score.

Body. Waist circumference predicts mortality even after accounting for BMI (Cerhan 2014). The common reference values (94 cm for men and 80 for women, and also 102 and 88) come from European studies and from IDF and NIH definitions, and they vary by ancestry. They are not a line between healthy and sick, and your own value is best understood together with a doctor. We did not find clear evidence that a DEXA scan adds to waist circumference.

Molecular level. Epigenetic clocks and telomere length are heavily researched, but in a single person they are noisy. On repeat testing of the same blood, the technical deviation reached up to 8.6 years in one clock (median 2.4 years; Higgins-Chen 2022). So the site has no such score.

All the layers: what goes into the calculation and what does not

The calculation on the site includes: biological age from blood (PhenoAge) when all nine values from the last year are present, VO2max if entered, walking pace and waist circumference. It does not include: the home tests below, a DEXA scan, visceral fat, epigenetic clocks and telomeres. There is no overall score and no weights.

Before trying tests at home: when not to do them

Do not do any of the home tests if any of these applies to you, and ask a doctor or a physiotherapist first.

Chest pain or pressure on exertion, or a diagnosis of heart disease. Uncontrolled high blood pressure. Dizziness, fainting or unsteadiness when standing up. A fall in the past year. Pregnancy. Osteoporosis. Recent surgery or fracture, or a new joint or back injury.

Stop immediately if pain, dizziness or unusual shortness of breath appears.

Tests you can do at home

The results are affected by tiredness, joint pain and practice, so they are not an accurate measure. They are meant to start a conversation with a doctor or a physiotherapist, not to compare or to get a score. They have no score, and no value from them goes into the calculation. Everything here is general information, not medical advice.

Waist circumference. Measure at navel height, after a normal exhale. It is a sign of belly fat, not a diagnosis. A value that worries you is a reason to talk to a doctor.

Home blood pressure. With an approved device, over several days in a row. It shows a pattern, not a diagnosis. High values that keep recurring are worth showing to a doctor.

Five chair stands. A stable chair without wheels, against a wall. It shows leg strength on that day. Unusual difficulty or dizziness: stop and ask a doctor.

One-leg stand. Next to a wall or a heavy table, not next to a chair on wheels. It shows a picture of balance on that day, not the risk of falling. Anyone who fell in the past year should not do this test.

Wall push-ups. Not to failure, and without holding your breath. Pain in the shoulder or chest: stop.

Comfortable-pace walk. This is not a maximal test. Anyone taking medication that changes heart rate, or in hot weather: skip it. It gives a rough estimate of fitness, not VO2max.

Grip. Only if you have a grip dynamometer. It is a rough estimate, and should not be compared with tables.

Expensive private tests: what the evidence says

Many people hear about private tests and ask whether they are worth it. We neither recommend them nor warn against them. Here is what was found, and the question worth asking a doctor: will the result change any decision of mine?

Epigenetic clock. On repeat testing of the same blood, differences of up to 8.6 years were found (median 2.4; Higgins-Chen 2022), so the gap between people is sometimes smaller than the noise.

DEXA scan. A body composition test that involves a small dose of radiation. We did not find clear evidence that it predicts mortality beyond waist circumference.

Continuous glucose monitor for people without diabetes. We did not find a trial showing an improvement in health outcomes.

Before paying for a test like this, it is worth asking a doctor what it would change in decisions.

When to see a doctor

Chest pain, dizziness or fainting are a reason to see a doctor, not to wait for a home test. Also, high values that keep recurring on home measurement (blood pressure, waist circumference) are a reason to talk.

Myth or fact

"My biological age is my true age"What do we know?

What we know: It is a risk measure based on group averages, not a measurement of the body's "age".

"A low biological age guarantees a long life"What do we know?

What we know: PhenoAge predicted mortality only slightly better than age alone (AUC 0.879 versus 0.855), and it is an estimate for groups.
