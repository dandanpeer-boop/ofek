Weight and BMI

The short answer: A BMI of 18.5–25 was linked to the lowest risk. In men, overweight was linked to a 12% higher risk, and grade 2 obesity to a risk 2.68 times higher.

The link between weight and mortality looks like a U: risk is lowest in the healthy BMI range and rises on both sides, especially with obesity. The data here come from a pooled analysis of 239 cohort studies with over 10 million people, so they are among the best established in the field.

How excess weight is defined

According to the World Health Organization, a BMI below 18.5 is underweight, 18.5 to 24.9 is the normal range, 25 to 29.9 is overweight, and 30 or more is obesity: grade 1 (30–34.9), grade 2 (35–39.9) and grade 3 (40 and above). This is the division used in the study, so the numbers here refer to these groups.

What the data say

The Global BMI Mortality Collaboration (Lancet 2016) analyzed people who never smoked and had no chronic disease, excluding the first five years of follow-up. Compared with a BMI of 18.5 to 25, the risk of death from any cause was: in men 1.12 with overweight, and 1.70, 2.68 and 4.24 with grade 1, 2 and 3 obesity. In women: 1.08, 1.37, 1.86 and 2.73.

How to read a hazard ratio

A hazard ratio of 1.12 means that at every age, the death rate in the group is 12% higher than in the group within the normal range. It does not mean that 12% of people will die. The closer the ratio is to 1, the smaller the difference; a ratio of 2.68 means 2.68 times the rate.

An example of reading

A man or a woman with a BMI of 32 is in grade 1 obesity, so the grade 1 ratio for their sex applies, as given in "What the data say". These are group hazard ratios and not a personal forecast, and the calculator combines them with age, smoking, blood tests and the other factors.

What the study did not examine

The study followed people over time and did not examine what happens to those who lose weight. So it cannot be concluded from it that losing weight will reduce the risk by a certain amount. The estimate in years in the calculator assumes that someone whose weight falls takes on the risk of someone at that weight, and that is an assumption.

What this means in years of life

A hazard ratio is not years. It multiplies the risk of dying at each age, and converting it to years depends on age, sex and other factors. That is why the calculator does it for you: change the weight and see the difference. Any figure in years is the model's estimate, not a result reported in a study.

A realistic target: a small change you can keep up over time is better than an "ideal" target that is hard to meet. You can check in the calculator how many years such a change is worth.

Risk falls with age

For every 5 BMI units above 25, risk rose by 51% in men and 30% in women. By age (combined estimate): 1.52 at 35–49, 1.37 at 50–69 and 1.21 at 70–89. The effect is stronger at a younger age, so it pays to pay attention early.

Being too light also carries risk

At a BMI below 18.5 the risk was 1.83 in men and 1.53 in women. Part of this comes from illness that causes weight loss, so someone losing weight without a clear reason needs a check-up, not a compliment.

Limits of BMI

BMI does not distinguish fat from muscle, and does not show where the fat is. Waist circumference adds information, which is why it is also in the calculator. All the numbers here come from observational studies, not from a trial in which people lost weight.

When to talk to a doctor

It is worth talking to a doctor when the BMI is 30 or more, when weight rises or falls quickly for no clear reason, or when excess weight comes together with high blood pressure or high blood sugar. A doctor can assess the whole picture and suggest suitable treatment.

Why smokers and the sick are filtered out

Low weight is often linked to high mortality, but not always because of the weight: smoking makes people leaner, and serious illness causes weight loss. If these people are left in the analysis, heavier people look healthier than they really are. So the study excluded those who smoked and those with a chronic disease, and also the first five years of follow-up. This reduces the bias, but does not remove it completely.

Waist circumference adds information

In a pooled study by Cerhan (Mayo Clin Proc 2014), every additional 5 cm of waist circumference was linked to an increase of about 7% to 9% in mortality risk. The figure is taken from the study abstract. That is why the calculator also asks about waist circumference: it helps to tell apart people with the same BMI.

How it enters the calculator

BMI enters according to the GBMC curve. In men the curve is adjusted upward from a BMI of 21, so that it matches the ratios reported for men. In the "what if" simulator you can move the weight and see how many years a change of a few kilograms is worth in your case. Any result in years is the model's estimate.

Myth or fact

"A little extra weight always protects"What do we know?

What we know: In a group with no disease and no smoking, even mild overweight was linked to higher risk (1.12 in men).

"BMI is a perfect measure"What do we know?

What we know: It is crude. Waist circumference, blood sugar and blood pressure complete the picture.
