Movement and fitness

The short answer: The bar is 150–300 minutes of moderate activity a week, per the World Health Organization. In the top step-count quartile (about 10,900 a day), mortality was 53% lower than in the bottom quartile (about 3,500).

Of all the things you can change, physical fitness is one of the most powerful. You don't need to become an athlete: the biggest gain is for people who go from almost nothing to a little.

The guideline

The World Health Organization recommends that adults do 150–300 minutes a week of moderate-intensity activity (brisk walking, cycling), or 75–150 minutes of vigorous activity, plus regular strength training. Some is better than none.

Steps

In a meta-analysis of 15 cohorts (Paluch 2022), mortality risk fell as daily steps rose, up to a plateau: about 6,000–8,000 steps a day over age 60, and about 8,000–10,000 under 60.

Strength, not just cardio

In a review of 16 studies (Momma 2022), strength training was linked to 10%–17% lower mortality risk, with the greatest benefit around 30–60 minutes a week. Combining strength and aerobic exercise was linked to the lowest risk.

Muscle and balance with age

Muscle mass and strength decline gradually with age, and in extreme cases this is called sarcopenia. According to a review by Abellan van Kan (2009), it affects 8% to 40% of people aged 60 and over, depending on the definition and the way it is measured, and there is still no agreed definition. The World Health Organization recommends muscle-strengthening activity at every age, and at least two days a week at moderate intensity or higher, covering all the major muscle groups.

After 65 there is also the matter of balance: at least a third of people this age fall every year. In a Cochrane review of 108 randomised trials with 23,407 participants (Sherrington 2019), exercise reduced the rate of falls by 23% (RaR 0.77, 95% CI 0.71–0.83). That is why the World Health Organization guidelines for people aged 65 and over include varied activity that emphasises balance and strength, on at least three days a week.

Aerobic fitness

In Kodama's meta-analysis (JAMA 2009), each one-MET increase in aerobic capacity was linked to 13% lower mortality. Fitness watches estimate this as VO2max, and you can enter the value in the calculator.

What about the sauna?

The best-known study comes from Finland (Laukkanen, 2015): about 2,300 men aged 42–60 were followed for about 20 years. Compared with those who used a sauna once a week, those who went 4–7 times a week died at a rate about 40% lower over that period, and sudden cardiac death was more than 60% rarer. A follow-up study (2018) found a similar link with heart disease deaths in women too.

Why this isn't a recommendation yet: these are observational studies, not trials. People with time for a sauna almost every day differ in other ways too, and the 4–7 times a week group had only about 200 men. The sauna itself, a traditional Finnish one at 80 to 100 degrees Celsius, isn't necessarily like every sauna. Short trials found lower blood pressure and more flexible blood vessels, which makes the link plausible, but doesn't yet prove a sauna extends life.

If you enjoy it, there's no reason to give it up. Drink water and don't go in after alcohol. Anyone with unstable heart disease, low blood pressure, or who is pregnant should talk to a doctor first.

Myth or fact

"You need 10,000 steps, or it's not worth it"What do we know?

What we know: Over age 60 the benefit levels off around 6,000–8,000 steps, and there is benefit well before that.

"Strength training is only for the young"What do we know?

What we know: The guidelines recommend strength training at every age, and the benefit was found already at 30–60 minutes a week.
