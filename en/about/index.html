About the site

How it works

The guides

Each guide is based on studies published in medical journals and on guidelines from bodies such as the World Health Organization. Every number on the site links to its source, and the numbers were checked against the studies' own abstracts. When a study is observational, meaning it shows an association and not necessarily a cause, that is stated.

The calculator

The starting point is the World Health Organization life table by country, age and sex. Each risk factor is translated into a hazard ratio from a large study and multiplies the death rate at every age. The effect weakens at older ages, and related factors count with reduced weight so the same thing is not counted twice. The result is shown as a range, because nobody can know a date.

Limits of the model

  • The model combines findings from separate studies. It is not built on one study tested against real outcomes, so it is not a validated model.
  • Factors that are linked, such as weight, blood sugar and blood pressure, partly overlap. The handling of this overlap is an approximation and may overstate or understate the result, especially for people with many risk factors.
  • The mortality table reflects mortality today, not future medical progress, so the estimate for younger people is probably conservative.
  • The range shown reflects differences between people with the same profile, not the uncertainty of the model itself.
  • Most studies are observational. When they show an association, it is not always clear how much of it is causal, so “how many years you could gain” is an estimate, not a promise.
  • Many of the studies were done in Western countries. The model does not know about rare diseases, genetics or acute risks.
  • In a first check we ran on US NHANES data (about 23,500 adults, 10-year follow-up), the model separated people at high and low risk well, but on average it predicted about 15 to 20 percent more deaths than were actually observed. The absolute estimate may therefore be somewhat too low, and comparisons between people or between habits are more reliable than the number itself. This is not an independent check, and it was not done on an Israeli population.

Ongoing updates

Every week PubMed is scanned for new research in the field: meta-analyses, systematic reviews, controlled trials and large cohort studies on mortality and life expectancy. Each study found is checked against its abstract and against the numbers already on the site, and only a strong study that changes the picture updates a number in the calculator or a guide. Not every new study changes anything, and that is intentional: a single unusual study should not move your estimate.

Privacy

Your answers aren't sent anywhere. The calculation, and the reading of blood test files and watch files, all happen in the browser. If you choose to remember your data, it is stored only in this browser, and you can delete it at any time with the "Delete my data" button at the bottom of the page. To know how many people visit the site and which pages, we use Google Analytics. It receives the page address and general details such as device type and country, not what you entered in the calculator. You can check this yourself: once the page has loaded, you can disconnect from the internet or switch to airplane mode and the calculator keeps working, including reading a blood test file.

Who built this site

The site was built by a private person. There is no insurance company, supplement company or profit motive behind it at this point.

The reason: after my mother died at 77, and cancer was found in my father at 77, I decided to help people improve their life expectancy.

The site was written with the help of artificial intelligence tools. Every number was checked against the abstract or full text of the original study.

What it is not

It is not a diagnosis and not medical advice. The studies describe averages of large groups, not the future of any one person. For any question about your health, talk to your doctor.
