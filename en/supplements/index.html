Dietary supplements

The short answer: For most supplements there is no evidence that they extend life in healthy people: in the VITAL trial vitamin D did not change overall mortality, and antioxidant supplements slightly raised it.

Dietary supplements are sold as a shortcut to a long life, but in the large trials almost none of them extended life in healthy people. There are exceptions, mainly when a deficiency is measured in a blood test.

The first rule: a supplement fills a deficiency

The important distinction is between a deficiency and an extra. For someone missing a vitamin or mineral, a supplement corrects the deficiency, and that can help. Someone without a deficiency gains almost no years in the large trials. So the first step is not to buy a supplement, but to check with a blood test and talk to a doctor.

Vitamin D

In the VITAL trial (Manson 2019), 25,871 participants received vitamin D or placebo for a median of 5.3 years. No difference was found in cancer (HR 0.96, 95% CI: 0.88–1.06), in cardiovascular events (HR 0.97, 0.85–1.12), or in death from any cause (HR 0.99, 0.87–1.12). Someone with a deficiency found in a blood test should decide on a supplement together with their doctor.

Omega-3 (fish oil)

In the Cochrane review (Abdelhamid 2020), 86 trials with 162,796 participants, omega-3 supplements barely changed overall mortality (RR 0.97, 95% CI: 0.93–1.01, high certainty). There was a small reduction in death from heart disease and in coronary events (RR 0.91 for coronary events), but certainty was low to moderate: about 167 people would need to be treated to prevent one coronary event.

Multivitamins and antioxidants

The USPSTF recommendation (2022) concluded, with moderate certainty, that the harms of beta-carotene outweigh its benefits in preventing heart disease and cancer, and that vitamin E offers no net benefit. For multivitamins and other single supplements, the evidence is insufficient to say whether benefits outweigh harms.

In Bjelakovic's meta-analysis (2007), 47 high-quality trials with 180,938 participants, antioxidant supplements slightly raised mortality (RR 1.05, 95% CI: 1.02–1.08). Beta-carotene (RR 1.07), vitamin A (RR 1.16) and vitamin E (RR 1.04) raised mortality, alone or combined. No effect on mortality was found for vitamin C or selenium. This is a 2007 study.

Vitamin B12: when yes

B12 is a case where testing and a supplement are sometimes justified. In the DPPOS trial (Aroda 2016), among people who took metformin for years, low B12 (up to 203 pg/mL) was more common than in the placebo group after 5 years (4.3% vs 2.3%), and low or borderline B12 was even more common (19.1% vs 9.5%). Each year of treatment added risk (OR 1.13 per year). The researchers recommended considering routine B12 testing for people on metformin. If a low value is found, the doctor will decide on treatment and dose.

Creatine: evidence for muscle, not for longevity

In Chilibeck's meta-analysis (2017), 22 trials with 721 participants aged 57–70 on average, creatine supplementation alongside resistance training increased lean muscle mass by 1.37 kg (95% CI: 0.97–1.76) and slightly increased strength. This is a measure of muscle, not of mortality: the studies did not test whether it extends life. The training itself is what matters most.

What about NR, NMN, resveratrol and others

Supplements such as nicotinamide riboside (NR) are marketed as anti-aging supplements. In Martens' trial (2018), two 6-week periods in healthy middle-aged and older adults, NR was well tolerated and raised NAD+ metabolism. This is a biological marker, and the study did not look at mortality or disease. The researchers themselves write that further trials are needed to test benefit, for example on blood pressure.

Safety

A supplement is not safe just because it is called natural. Supplements can affect medications and underlying conditions, and they usually do not go through efficacy approval the way a drug does. Before you start, especially with regular medication, a chronic condition, pregnancy or breastfeeding, it is worth asking a doctor or pharmacist.
